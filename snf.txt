It is possible, and the most valuable aspect of your research topic is identifying "which patients, under what home support conditions, can use home health (HH) as an alternative to a Skilled Nursing Facility (SNF) while maintaining health outcomes." Your idea about prior home health experience is highly valuable, but I would reframe it from "frequent usage" to "a proven track record of stable recovery with home care support."

Below are the research hypotheses I would prioritize, based on current literature. These are directions waiting to be validated and should not be treated as established conclusions upfront.

Calibrating Overall Expectations
Your intuition is supported by some evidence. A 2019 study by Werner et al. in JAMA Internal Medicine analyzed approximately 17.24 million hospitalizations. Using an instrumental variable approach, they found that discharging to home health compared to an SNF resulted in:

A 5.6 percentage point higher 30-day readmission rate;

No significant differences in mortality or functional recovery;

An average reduction of about $4,514 in total 60-day Medicare payments, calculated from the initial hospital admission. (PubMed)

Therefore, a more accurate expectation is that, on average, there may be a trade-off between "cost savings" and "increased readmissions." However, this does not mean that all health outcomes will inevitably worsen, nor can we infer that this applies to all subgroups. Instrumental variable results also rely on their underlying assumptions and cannot be directly applied to every individual patient originally slated for an SNF.

1. Your Idea on Prior HH Experience: Focus on "Successful Experience + Continuity of Care"
The underlying mechanism makes sense: patients and their families are familiar with the care process, and the agency understands the home environment, medications, and functional baselines, making the reactivation of services smoother.

Existing research indicates that higher continuity of care providers in home health is associated with fewer hospitalizations, fewer emergency visits, and better activities of daily living (ADL) function. Studies targeting dementia patients have also found an association between care continuity and fewer readmissions. However, this is indirect support and does not directly prove that "people with high historical HH usage are safer switching to HH over SNF." (PubMed)

I recommend breaking down the historical records into specific, testable hypotheses:

No short-term readmissions or transfers to SNF after previous HH: Indicates a successful history of home recovery; these patients might be better suited for alternative care.

Recently receiving HH, with stable services prior to current admission: Existing care relationships may reduce friction during the transition.

Past HH concentrated within the same agency: The agency's familiarity with the patient might be advantageous over frequently changing agencies.

Past HH service type matches current needs: For example, successfully completing similar post-operative rehab in the past.

Recurrent ER visits, readmissions, or SNF transfers after past HH: Could imply that past home support was insufficient, making the substitution riskier.

"High volume of HH" could also signal severe frailty and unstable chronic conditions. Therefore, I am more optimistic about a "trajectory of past success" rather than the raw number of visits or costs. Using the same agency is merely a proxy for care continuity, not a guarantee of having the exact same nurse.

2. Elective, Uncomplicated Hip/Knee Replacements: Highest Priority Among Diseases
Your point that "it's mainly recovery and may not require intensive daily nursing" is best supported by literature on carefully selected elective joint replacement patients.

A 2020 study in JAMA Network Open used matching and difference-in-differences methods to study around 190,000 patients. Following payment policy changes that shifted more patients to home recovery, the study found no increase in surgical complications or mortality, and readmissions actually decreased. However, it compared "home care (with or without HH)" against "institutional care (including SNFs)," rather than a pure HH vs. SNF comparison. (JAMA Network)

Subgroups worth prespecifying include:

Elective surgery without major post-operative complications;

Independent or largely independent living before admission;

Ability to safely transfer and walk prior to discharge;

Available family assistance to ensure home rehabilitation can be executed.

Note: Do not lump elective joint replacements together with hip fractures caused by falls. They should be treated separately in research, as the baseline function, frailty, and acute functional loss of fracture patients can be entirely different.

3. "Minimal Functional Decline" May Identify Opportunities Better Than "Disease Name"
This is an angle I believe you might be underestimating. Two people may both have pneumonia, but one lived independently prior to admission and merely experienced a drop in stamina at discharge, while the other remains unable to get out of bed or use the toilet independently. The same diagnosis does not equate to the same care needs.

My mechanistic hypothesis is: for patients who are medically stable, have functional abilities close to their pre-admission baseline, and primarily need intermittent rehabilitation, the marginal benefit of an SNF over HH is likely small. Joint replacement studies suggest that patients with better physical condition and social support fare well with home care, offering some backing for this direction; however, extending this to other diseases still requires validation.

Prioritize analyzing the following metrics prior to the discharge decision:

Walking, bed-to-chair transfer, and toileting abilities;

The difference between pre-admission and current ADLs;

PT/OT evaluations regarding the level of assistance needed;

Cognitive, swallowing, and medication management capabilities.

If you only have claims data, you can use proxies like frailty indices, assistive devices, past falls, and utilization history, though these are poor substitutes for true functional assessments.

4. Strong Family Caregiver Capacity: The Crucial Interaction Term
The key here is not merely being "married" or "living with family," but rather: is there someone capable, willing, and available to fill the caregiving gaps when professionals are not in the home?

A 2025 study on dementia HH patients found that inadequate family caregiver capacity in medical, functional, or daily tasks was associated with varying utilization of professional services. This supports measuring caregiver capacity independently, though it cannot definitively prove it is an effect modifier for the HH vs. SNF decision just yet. (ScienceDirect)

I would focus on testing whether moderate functional limitations + reliable family support is better at identifying safe substitution candidates than "low disease risk" alone. This data can be extracted from case management or discharge planning records: has a caregiver been confirmed? What specific help can they provide? How many hours a day can they support? Do home stairs or equipment limitations pose barriers?

5. Timely Service Initiation: The "Patient + Service Bundle"
For the same individual, having care initiate shortly after discharge versus waiting several days to start HH can yield vastly different outcomes. Observational studies on joint replacement and diabetic patients have found that delayed HH initiation correlates with poorer outcomes (e.g., initiating care 3–7 days post-discharge resulted in higher readmission odds compared to starting within the first two days). (PubMed)

Therefore, alternative care models worth investigating include:

The agency accepting the referral prior to discharge;

Prompt initiation of nursing/rehabilitation;

Medications and essential equipment already in place;

Clear follow-up channels and protocols for escalating medical issues.

Analytical Crux: The actual time to first visit and the number of visits after the current discharge cannot be treated as baseline subgroup variables at the time of discharge. They belong to the treatment implementation process and should be evaluated as part of the intervention strategy or in a separate mechanistic analysis.

6. Dementia Patients: Evaluate Alongside Family Support and Behaviors
This might run counter to your intuition but remains an exploratory direction worth keeping. A 2022 study in Health Services Research analyzing around 978,000 dementia patients found no significant difference in readmissions or mortality between HH and SNF using instrumental variables. (PubMed)

The testable mechanistic hypothesis is: familiarity with the home environment might be beneficial, but heavy care needs could offset this advantage. Therefore, you should study Dementia severity/behavioral symptoms × Family caregiver capacity × Prior care continuity. Do not jump to conclusions based simply on the "presence or absence of dementia."

7. Mild-to-Moderate Stroke: Evidence for Home Rehab Exists
A Cochrane review on early supported discharge found that selected mild-to-moderate stroke patients, when backed by coordinated multidisciplinary teams, can experience reduced long-term dependency and institutional care.

This suggests that medically stable patients with limited functional loss and adequate family support might be suitable for home rehab. However, these services are typically much more systematic than standard HH. You cannot directly translate "team-supported home rehab is effective" to "routine HH can replace SNFs."

Proposed Prioritization for Initial Analysis
Priority	Prespecified Subgroup / Hypothesis	Rationale
1	Elective joint replacement + good function + family support	Closest alignment with existing comparative evidence.
2	Successful prior HH experience + stable agency relationship	Fits your data and original hypothesis well.
3	Minimal functional decline, primarily needing intermittent rehab	Strong cross-disease mechanistic explanation.
4	Adequate family caregiving + timely HH implementation	Could form actionable intervention protocols.
5	Dementia + stable family support / care relationships	Comparative studies exist, but requires deep segmentation.
6	Mild-to-moderate stroke + comprehensive home rehab support	Evidence exists, but standard service models may not match.
Finally, to ensure these hypotheses genuinely answer your business question, three parameters must be established from the outset:

Clearly define the "originally SNF-bound" population. It is best to have SNF recommendations, referrals, or prior authorization records. Simply comparing all patients who actually went to HH versus those who went to an SNF will not answer "whether patients originally planned for an SNF can safely switch to HH."

Study the heterogeneous treatment effects, not just readmission risk. You are looking for patients where "the additional risk of HH compared to SNF is minimal." Low-risk patients might still not benefit from SNF, while high-risk patients won't necessarily always benefit more from SNF.

"No significant increase" does not mean "non-inferior in safety." Predefine clinically acceptable non-inferiority margins and use confidence intervals to judge; any exploratory subgroups found must be independently validated. Additionally, look at mortality, ED/observation visits, functional recovery, and subsequent institutional transfers, while calculating total 60/90-day costs to avoid merely observing the disappearance of the SNF fee.

The core hypothesis I would prioritize validating is this: Patients who have a history of successful home care, limited functional loss this time around, and confirmed home support are much more likely to achieve a safe, cost-saving substitution than those who simply "use HH a lot" or have a "low predicted readmission risk."
